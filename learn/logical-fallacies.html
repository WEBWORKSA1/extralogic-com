15 Logical Fallacies Everyone Should Know

A fallacy is an error in reasoning that makes an argument look stronger than it is. Learn to spot these 15 and you'll read the news, meetings and comment sections differently.

1. Ad hominem

Attacking the person instead of the argument. "You can't trust her climate data — she drives an SUV." Respond: "Is the data itself wrong?"

2. Straw man

Distorting someone's claim into a weaker version and then attacking that. "You want better bike lanes? So you want to ban cars?"

3. False dilemma

Presenting two options when more exist. "Either we cut the budget in half or the company goes bankrupt."

4. Slippery slope

Claiming one step will inevitably lead to an extreme outcome, without evidence for each link in the chain.

5. Appeal to authority

Treating a claim as true because an authority said so, especially when that authority is outside their field.

6. Circular reasoning

The conclusion is hidden in the premise. "This book is reliable because it says it is reliable."

7. Hasty generalization

Drawing a broad conclusion from too few examples. "Two of my customers complained, so everyone hates the update."

8. Post hoc ergo propter hoc

Assuming that because B followed A, A caused B. Correlation isn't causation.

9. Appeal to popularity (bandwagon)

"Millions of people believe it, so it must be true."

10. Red herring

Bringing in an irrelevant topic to distract from the real issue.

11. Tu quoque (whataboutism)

Deflecting criticism by pointing to the critic's own behavior, which doesn't make the original point wrong.

12. Sunk cost fallacy

Continuing something because of what you've already invested rather than what you'll get from here on.

13. Affirming the consequent

If P then Q. Q. Therefore P. Invalid: the street being wet doesn't prove it rained.

14. Appeal to emotion

Using fear, pity or outrage in place of evidence.

15. No true Scotsman

Redefining a group to dodge a counterexample. "No real programmer uses a mouse."

Quick self-check

"We've spent $2M on this project — we can't stop now." Which fallacy?

Sunk cost fallacy.

"Sales rose after we changed the logo, so the logo boosted sales." Which fallacy?

Post hoc ergo propter hoc: other causes (season, pricing, campaigns) aren't ruled out.
